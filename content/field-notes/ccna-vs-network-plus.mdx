If you're about to sink a few hundred dollars and a few months of evenings into a networking cert, you've probably narrowed it to two: CompTIA Network+ and Cisco's CCNA. They usually get compared as if one were the easy version of the other. We don't think that's the useful way to look at them.

Network+ is vendor-neutral and covers a lot of ground. It wants to know whether you understand how networks work on anyone's equipment, including the parts you'll never touch from a keyboard, like cabling, wireless, cloud models, and documentation. CCNA covers less ground but goes much deeper, and it assumes the network in front of you runs Cisco IOS. Most of the other differences follow from that.

## The one-screen comparison

| | CompTIA Network+ | Cisco CCNA |
|---|---|---|
| Vendor focus | Vendor-neutral | Cisco, including the IOS command line |
| Current exam | N10-009 (launched June 2024) | 200-301 v1.1 |
| Next revision | Expected around 2027 | v2.0, starting February 3, 2027 |
| Length | 90 minutes, up to 90 questions | 120 minutes (Cisco does not publish a question count) |
| Question style | Multiple choice plus performance-based questions | Not officially broken down; candidates report multiple choice, drag-and-drop, and simulations |
| Passing score | 720 on a 100-900 scale | Not published |
| Price (US list, September 2026) | Around $400 | $300 |
| Recommended background | CompTIA A+ plus 9 to 12 months in a junior network role | No formal prerequisites |
| Valid for | 3 years | 3 years |
| Renewal | 30 CEUs and $150 in CE fees per cycle | 30 CE credits, a retake, or a higher Cisco cert |

Prices change, so check the CompTIA store and Cisco's exam page before you buy.

## What each exam actually tests

CompTIA splits N10-009 into five domains: Networking Concepts (23%), Network Implementation (20%), Network Operations (19%), Network Security (14%), and Network Troubleshooting (24%). Besides subnetting and routing, you're expected to know cable categories and connectors, wireless standards and channels, cloud service models, documentation, change management, and disaster recovery. A fair amount of that is recall.

CCNA 200-301 v1.1 has six areas: network fundamentals, network access, IP connectivity, IP services, security fundamentals, and automation and programmability. On paper it looks like a similar list. In practice, knowing what a VLAN is gets you nowhere on CCNA. You have to create one, trunk it, route between VLANs, bring up OSPF, write an ACL, and configure NAT, all in IOS syntax, and then read the `show` output that proves it worked.

The two share more than you'd guess. The OSI model, subnetting, VLANs and trunking, basic static and dynamic routing, DHCP, DNS, ACLs, and NAT all show up on both, so studying for one gets you a good way into the other.

## Vendor-neutral versus the Cisco CLI

You'll feel this one on exam day.

Network+ never asks for a vendor's syntax. Its performance-based questions drop you into a simplified simulation and ask you to configure, match, or fix something, and what gets graded is whether you understood the problem.

CCNA grades the typing too. You're expected to know that `switchport mode trunk` makes a trunk and that `show ip interface brief` is the first thing you run. If you want to work at a Cisco shop (and plenty of shops are), that's time well spent. If you end up on Juniper or Arista instead, the concepts carry over and most of the exact commands don't.

## Difficulty

CCNA is the harder exam, and we don't think it's close. There's more material, it goes deeper, your subnetting has to be fast as well as right, and recalling config with a clock running is a skill of its own.

Network+ still isn't a gimme. The breadth means a lot of memorizing: port numbers, cable categories, wireless standards, the steps of a change-management process. And the performance-based questions catch people who memorized without understanding. If you've done a year of hands-on networking, Network+ will probably feel comfortable and CCNA will feel like real work. If you're coming from help desk with no networking background, Network+ on its own is plenty.

## Cost and keeping it

At list price CCNA is cheaper: $300 against roughly $400 for Network+. Honestly, that $100 is the least important number in the table. A failed attempt generally means buying another voucher, so passing the first time moves your real cost far more than the sticker does.

Both are valid for three years. Renewing Network+ means earning 30 continuing education units in the cycle and paying CompTIA $150 in CE fees across the three years. Renewing CCNA means earning 30 Cisco continuing education credits, retaking the current exam, or moving up to a higher Cisco certification, which renews CCNA as a side effect.

## The 2027 problem: both exams are about to change

If you're starting today, circle this date before you worry about anything else.

Cisco announced CCNA 200-301 v2.0 in May 2026. The last day to sit v1.1 is February 2, 2027, and v2.0 starts the next day. The exam code stays 200-301, but the blueprint gets reorganized from six domains into five: Network Infrastructure and Connectivity (25%), Switching and Network Access (25%), IP Routing (20%), Network Services and Security (20%), and AI, Network Operations and Management (10%). The more interesting change is in the verbs. A lot of topics that used to say describe or configure now say troubleshoot or diagnose, and DNS and DHCP troubleshooting, Ansible, and AI-assisted operations are new.

Our advice: if you already have a few months of study behind you, book v1.1 before February 2. If you're starting from zero, study for v2.0 and don't spend time on topics it drops.

Network+ is on a similar clock. CompTIA usually retires an exam about three years after launch, and N10-009 is expected to go around 2027.

This is less scary than it sounds. A revision reshuffles the weights and adds a handful of topics. Subnetting, VLANs, routing, and DHCP will be on whatever version comes next, so if you learn those properly, a new blueprint means catching up on a few topics and nothing more.

## Which should you take first

Here's our call.

If you're new to IT or coming from help desk, and you want a real networking foundation without committing to network engineering yet, take Network+. It's the natural next step after A+, and it lines up with Security+ if security is where you're heading.

If you want to be a network engineer or admin, and the employers you're looking at run Cisco, take CCNA. If you already understand the fundamentals, you can skip Network+ entirely.

If you want network engineering but networking itself is new to you, it's closer. Network+ first is the gentler route, and the overlap means you start CCNA prep well ahead. If you can only pay for one exam, we'd go straight to CCNA and budget extra study time.

Plenty of engineers end up holding both, usually Network+ first.
